A Snapshot of Female-headed Establishments in India: Key Insights from Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises 2025

Gender disaggregated analysis on growth, sectors, states, registration and access to credit.

By Manya Aggarwal, Phalasha Nagpal and Gaurav Kumar Sinha

Insight 01

Female-headed establishments make up 28.6% of India’s proprietary establishments, yet few of them are hired-worker establishments;  a pattern consistent across all three broad industries.

Within India's 74.9 million proprietary establishments, 21.4 million are female-headed. Among these, only 3.0% are female-led hired-worker establishments (HWEs), against 16.8% of male-led HWEs. The HWE gap is widest in manufacturing, where female establishments even outnumber male-headed ones. Hence, female-headed establishments are growing, yet the majority remain own-account establishments (OAEs).
1

The base of total proprietary establishments (male and female) grew 21.6% from 2022-23 to 2025 yet almost all of it was own-account.


Proprietary establishments rose from 61.6 million in 2022–23 to 74.9 million in 2025, an addition of 13.3 million (+21.6%). Female-headed establishments grew by 43.6% as against 14.6% growth in male-headed ones.

2

28.6% of proprietary establishments are female-headed

There are 21.4 million (28.6%) female-headed proprietary establishments against 53.5 million male-headed proprietary establishments in 2025.For every five male-headed establishments, there are about two female-headed establishments.

3

Only 3.0% are female-led hired-worker establishments

In absolute numbers, only 634.2K (3.0%) female-headed proprietary establishments are hired-worker establishments (HWEs), against 9.0 million (16.8%) of male-led HWEs.Male-headed establishments are 5.6× as likely to be HWEs.

4.1
Manufacturing | By gender

Female-headed establishments outnumber male-headed ones yet witness the widest HWE gap of any industry.

Female-headed manufacturing establishments outnumber male-headed ones (12.9 million vs. 8.4 million), about 1.5 times as many, or three in every five. Only 1.2% of the female-headed establishments are HWEs, against 25.0% of the male-headed ones.
Men's businesses are 20.8 times as likely to employ workers as women's, the widest gap of the three industries.
4.2
Trade | By gender

Only 13.4% of trade establishments are female-headed

There are 3.2 million female-headed trade establishments against 20.7 million male-headed, or 13.4% of the total trade establishments. Only 4.3% of female-headed establishments are HWEs, against 17.9% male-led HWEs. 
Men's businesses are 4.2 times as likely to employ workers as women's, a wider gap than in other services but narrower than in manufacturing.
4.3
Other Services | By gender

Roughly one in six other services establishments are female-headed, with the narrowest hiring gap

There are 5.2 million female-headed other services establishments against 24.4 million male-headed. Only 6.6% of them are female-led HWEs, against 13.1% male-led HWE.Men's businesses are twice as likely to employ workers as women's, the narrowest gap of the three industries.

Insight 02

Female-headed establishments accounted for 48.9% of the net increase between 2022–23 and 2025

Between 2022–23 and 2025, proprietary establishments recorded a net increase of 13.3 million, of which 6.5 million (48.9%) were female-headed, despite accounting for only 24.2% of total establishments in 2022–23. Among new establishments operating for up to three years, growth was similarly concentrated in own-account establishments. 
1
Growth since 2022–23

48.9% of net increase in proprietary establishments were female-headed

Of the 13.3 million proprietary establishments added between 2022–23 and 2025, 6.5 million (48.9%) were female-headed. 

Nearly all of the net increase was own-account: 98.0% in the female-headed, against 91% in male-headed.

Comparing only activities covered in both years, 10.0 million establishments were added, and 4.5 million (44.7%) were female-headed. The female-headed base grew 30.2%, against 12.0% for male-headed establishments. 97% of the female-headed addition was own-account, against 89% of the male-headed addition.

2
New establishments

Female-headed establishments are a growing share of new establishments

Among establishments up to three years of operation, 36.1% are female-headed, up from 30% in 2022–23. 

Nearly all (96.5%) of the female-headed establishments up to three years of operation are OAEs. 

Comparing only activities covered in both years, 35.5% of establishments up to three years of operation are female-headed, up from 30%. Female-headed establishments up to three years of operation grew 24%, while male-headed ones fell 3%.Six in ten are in manufacturing, led by wearing apparel

3
New female-headed establishments by sector

84.2% of new female-headed establishments are in just six sectors

More than half of the new female establishments up to three years of operation are in manufacturing, led by wearing apparel (1.9 million). Six top sectors include wearing apparel, retail trade, personal services*, education*, textiles and tobacco products.

Insight 03

Female-led hired-worker establishments generate 12.2 times more gross value added (GVA) per establishment than own-account establishments, yet employment generation and hired work are losing ground.

Average GVA per female-headed establishment rises from ₹68.3K as OAE to ₹8.3L as HWE (12.2× gap), close to the ₹8.8L of male-led HWEs. Yet, since 2022-23, hired workers grew only 10.0%, against 21.6% for establishments. The largest value gain available to female-headed establishments rests with the stage that is growing slowest.
1
As own-account

Female-led OAEs generate less than half the average GVA of male-led OAEs

Female-led OAEs generate an average annual GVA of ₹68.3K, compared to ₹1.8 lakh for male-led ones. The gap is widest in manufacturing, at ₹47.9K versus ₹1.7 lakh per establishment.

2
With workers

Female-headed establishments nearly match male-headed ones in average GVA when they hire workers


Female-led HWEs generate an average annual GVA of ₹8.3 lakh, compared to ₹8.8 lakh for male-led ones. In trade, female-led HWEs marginally outperform male-led ones, at ₹8.5 lakh versus ₹8.4 lakh per establishment.

3
The gap

Female-led HWEs generate 7 to 16 times more GVA than female-led OAEs, a wider gap than among male-headed ones


Across industries, female-led HWEs generate 12.2× the average GVA of own-account establishments, compared to 4.9× for male-led ones. The gap is widest in manufacturing (16.3× for female vs. 5.8× for male) and narrowest in trade (7.1× for female vs. 4.4× for male).

4
Establishments, workers and hired workers

Workers grew more slowly than establishments, and hired workers slower still

India's 74.9 million overall proprietary establishments employ 119.8 million workers, of whom only 26.5 million are hired workers. Since 2022–23, establishments grew by 21.6%, workers by 17.2% and hired workers hardly by 10.0%.

5
Hired and non-hired workers

Only about one in five workers is a hired worker

The hired-workers share slipped from 23.6% in 2022–23 to 22.1% in 2025. 

The non-hired workers include working owners, unpaid family members, helpers, apprentice, intern etc.

6
Across gender

Only 1 in 15 workers in female-headed establishments are hired, compared to 1 in 4 in male-headed ones


Female-headed establishments account for 26.3 million workers, of whom only 1.8 million are hired workers (6.8%). In comparison, 26.4% of workers in male-headed establishments are hired (24.7 million out of 93.5 million), nearly four times the share.

Udaiti's take

Women own nearly 30% of India’s unincorporated non-agricultural establishments, but growth in numbers has not translated into scale. Most remain micro or subsistence-driven, with only ~3% hiring workers, compared with ~17% of male-headed establishments. Some of this growth may be necessity-driven, reflecting limited wage employment opportunities, household income shocks and reliance on home-based work.

Within this base, growth-oriented female-headed establishments (GoFEs) - establishments with an established scale of operations, ambition and potential to grow - remain underserved due to gender inequities in access to finance, markets and growth support. Addressing these barriers can enable GoFEs to scale, driving greater investment, productivity and employment, particularly for women.

Insight 04 · All industries

The share of female-led hired worker establishments fell in two of three industries, and the gender gap barely moved

Female- and male-led HWEs both increased in absolute numbers, but slower than their overall establishment bases. As a result, the female-led HWE share fell from 3.4% to 3.0% and the male-led HWE share from 18.0% to 16.8%. The gender gap narrowed only marginally: from 14.6 to 13.8 percentage points as the HWE share weakened for both, and not because women’s share increased. The decline was sharper in manufacturing and other services, while trade remained broadly stable.

Insight 05 · Manufacturing

Six in ten female-headed establishments are in manufacturing, concentrated primarily in three sectors

Manufacturing accounts for 60.5% of female-headed proprietary establishments, yet female-led HWEs increased by only 16.1K between 2022–23 and 2025, with their share declining from 1.4% to 1.2%. The female-led HWE share fell in 8 of 15 manufacturing sectors, rose in 5 and was unchanged in 2.
5.1
Manufacturing
Female-headed establishments drove nine in ten new manufacturing establishments

Between 2022–23 and 2025, female-headed establishments accounted for 3.2 million of the 3.6 million net additions in manufacturing. Three sectors contributed 86.0% of these additions: wearing apparel (+2.0 million), textiles (+486.7K), and tobacco products (+264.5K).

5.2
Manufacturing

Female-headed establishments declined in only two sectors


Between 2022–23 and 2025, female-headed establishments recorded a net decline in two sectors: fabricated metal products (−8.9K) and beverages (−2.5K). In contrast, male-headed establishments in fabricated metal products increased by 144.2K over the same period.

Insight 06 · Trade

About 1.5 in 10 female-headed establishments are in trade, predominantly in retail.

Trade holds 15.2% of female-headed establishments. Retail dominates with 98.1% of them, but only 3.5% of female-headed retail establishments are HWEs. By contrast, wholesale holds only 1.7%, yet 37.1% of female-headed wholesale establishments are HWEs. Women are concentrated where trade hires the least and nearly absent where it hires the most.
6.1
Trade 

465.0K female-headed trade establishments were added, almost all in retail

Retail took 451.4K of them, or 97.1%. Female-headed establishments made up less than a third of all new trade establishments (465.0K of 1.6 million).

Insight 07 · Other Services

About 2.5 in 10 female-headed establishments are in other services industry: the fastest growing and most varied industry.

Other Services holds 24.3% of India’s female-headed establishments, up from 16.1% in 2022–23. Among service sectors, personal services added the most, almost all as OAEs. The sectors where female-led HWEs are most common, such as accommodation and human health, hold only a small share of female-headed establishments.
7.1
Other Services

Personal services alone added 2.0 million female-headed establishments

Other Services added 2.8 million female-headed establishments by 2025 in the 8.1 million total, with 71.4% (2.0 million) coming from personal services; education added another 287.1K and Food & Beverage Services 132.8K. Part of this increase reflects both actual establishment growth and expanded survey coverage, particularly in personal services, education and real estate.

7.2
Other Services

Female-headed establishments fell in only two sectors

Female-headed establishments fell in only 2 of the 15 sectors: rental and leasing and membership organisations. Male-headed establishments grew in both, by 70.9K and 90.1K.

Udaiti's take

Female-headed establishments are concentrated in manufacturing, followed by other services and trade. Despite manufacturing’s high employment potential, hiring remains low, with women concentrated in traditionally feminised industries such as apparel, textiles and food products.

In trade, women are concentrated in retail, where hiring is limited, with minimal presence in wholesale, where hiring is significantly higher. Similarly, growth in other services is driven largely by own-account personal services, while higher hiring in human health, accommodation and food and beverage services signals greater potential for employment generation.

Market-led approaches can help scale female-headed establishments where a market constraint is binding. In manufacturing, cluster development, enterprise aggregation and stronger value-chain linkages can expand hiring, open access to larger markets and enable a move into higher-value activities. In trade, stronger buyer-supplier linkages and access to working capital can support entry into wholesale. In services, digital market access and targeted business support can help enterprises reach wider markets and scale.

Insight 08 · Establishments by state

The states with the most female-led own account establishments are entirely different from the states with female-led hired-worker establishments providing employment

Uttar Pradesh leads on overall total proprietary establishments, West Bengal on female-headed establishments, and Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Kerala on female-led HWEs.
8.1
Total proprietary establishments

Uttar Pradesh has the most establishments of any state

Five outlined states hold nearly half (35.6 million, 47.5%) of India’s all proprietary establishments: Uttar Pradesh (leading), West Bengal, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Bihar

8.2
Female-headed establishments

West Bengal has the most female-headed establishments

The largest female-headed base is in West Bengal: 3.6M establishments, or 16.8% of India's total ahead of Uttar Pradesh (2.2 million) and Maharashtra (1.9 million)

8.3
OAEs and HWEs per 1,000 women

West Bengal has the most female-led OAEs per 1,000 women; the north-east and south India lead on female-led HWEs

India has 30.2 female-led own-account establishments for every 1,000 women. West Bengal, Manipur and Lakshadweep lead, with Telangana close behind. Insight 09 · HWE share by state

More female-headed establishments does not necessarily ensure more hired-worker establishments and greater employment generation

West Bengal has the largest female-headed base, yet only 0.9% of these establishments are HWEs. Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Maharashtra, Haryana and Assam combine a large base with an above-median HWE share. The size of a state's female-headed base says little about its share of female-led HWEs.
9.1
Female-headed base and HWE share

A larger base of female-headed establishments does not necessarily translate into a higher female-led HWE


States show considerable variation in the share of female-led hired-worker establishments. 

These differences may reflect variations in socioeconomic conditions, entrepreneurial networks, access to credit and markets, institutional support, and ease of doing business. The chart limits to highlighting these patterns but does not establish which factors explain them.

Udaiti's take

Opportunities and constraints for female-headed establishments vary across states, shaped by industrial and sectoral composition, labour supply and demand, demographics, gender norms, socioeconomic conditions, infrastructure and private-sector participation. This calls for context-specific, gender-responsive strategies, with state governments playing a central role in anchoring interventions, driving convergence and enabling impact at scale.

Insight 10 · Who is registered

Female-led OAEs have low registration rates across industries. The gender gap is smaller among hired-worker establishments, where female-led establishments lead in trade and other services.

Female-led OAEs have lower registration rates than male-led OAEs across industries, while female-led HWEs lead in trade and other services. Registration rises with establishment size where female-led HWEs with 15+ worker category leading across all three industries. Priority towards smaller female-led manufacturing HWEs is needed, where registartion rates remain below 50%.
10.1
Manufacturing

Only 3.7% of female-led OAEs are registered, compared to 23.3% of male-led OAEs


Manufacturing has the widest gender gap in OAE registration, despite 98.8% of female-led manufacturing establishments being OAEs. Among HWEs, female-led HWEs have higher registration rates than male-led HWEs in the 10–14 worker category and above.
10.2
Trade

Female-led OAEs trail male-led OAEs on registration, while the trend reverses for HWEs


23.7% female-led OAEs in trade are registered against 36.5% male-led OAEs. Among HWEs, registration rates are similar for both genders at 1–2 worker category, but higher for female-led HWEs at every other worker category, with the widest gap at 6–9 workers.

10.3
Other Services

Gender gap is observed in OAE registration across gender but among HWEs, female-led lead across most size bands as compared to male-led establishments

Udaiti's take

In most worker-size categories, the average institutional loan held by female-headed establishments remains lower than that held by male-headed establishments.

Female-headed establishments therefore remain largely dependent on microfinance, with limited access to larger loans for working capital and enterprise growth. Key barriers include limited awareness and information gaps, lack of collateral and guarantors, inadequate commercial evidence for credit assessment, and informality. Formal credit products and processes also often fail to adequately address the specific needs of female-headed establishments.

Limited access to formal credit remains a critical constraint to the growth and sustainability of female-headed establishments. With appropriate support, growth-oriented female-headed establishments can progressively access larger loans, expand operations, and generate additional employment opportunities, particularly for women, thereby enhancing their economic contribution.

What interventions can address these barriers and enable female-headed establishments to scale?

Conclusion

Udaiti's take

The constraint on women’s enterprise in India lies less in the number of establishments than in their ability to scale. The large existing base, particularly in manufacturing, offers significant job-creation potential. Yet, despite well-documented gender barriers, existing support remains fragmented and does not adequately address women’s specific needs and constraints. Addressing these gaps requires targeted, bundled and gender-responsive interventions, grounded in evidence and focused on measurable outcomes, such as turnover growth, and job creation.

Given the variation in outcomes across states, one approach is to anchor interventions in state governments, tailored to local contexts. Convening departments and ecosystem partners to deliver coordinated support can provide women entrepreneurs with integrated access to the resources needed to scale. Our work with MSInS in Maharashtra demonstrates this approach.

A second approach is to anchor interventions in a clearly defined market constraint and a committed market actor, testing gender-responsive solutions and scaling what works. Our experience shows that early government engagement can enable successful models to scale.

Beyond individual enterprises, these approaches can drive systemic change by embedding gender-responsive approaches within government programmes, market institutions and enterprise support ecosystems.
